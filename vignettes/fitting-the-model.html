How weave fits the model

This vignette walks through how weave fits its spatio-temporal model. The goal is to give you a working mental picture of every block of the sampler, without skipping the parts that make it correct. It assumes you are comfortable with vectors, matrices, and a small amount of probability; it does not assume any background in MCMC, Gaussian processes, or Bayesian computation.

We’ll cover, in order:

  1. The model and what we want from it.
  2. Why straightforward approaches don’t work, and what we do instead.
  3. The Pólya-Gamma identity — the one trick that makes the rest easy.
  4. The five blocks of one sampling sweep, explained one at a time.
  5. Multiple chains, diagnostics, and what the output actually means.

1. The model

We have weekly case counts \(y_{st}\) at health facility \(s\) in week \(t\). We want to do three things with them at once:

  • Smooth over time, so a single week’s spike doesn’t dominate.
  • Borrow strength across sites, so a quiet facility benefits from its neighbours.
  • Fill in missing weeks in a principled way, with honest uncertainty.

The model that does all three is:

\[ y_{st} \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(r, \mu = e^{\psi_{st}}), \qquad \psi_{st} = \mu_s + f_{st}, \]

with the latent field \(f\) given a Gaussian process prior:

\[ f \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \; K_{\text{space}} \otimes K_{\text{time}}). \]

In plain English:

  • Each count is negative binomial with mean \(\lambda_{st} = e^{\psi_{st}}\) and a dispersion parameter \(r\) (small \(r\) → heavy tails; \(r \to \infty\) → Poisson).
  • The log-mean \(\psi_{st}\) is a site intercept \(\mu_s\) plus a smooth latent surface \(f_{st}\).
  • The surface \(f\) has a Gaussian-process prior: it’s smooth in space and in time, with the smoothness controlled by three knobs (the kernel hyperparameters \(\theta\)):
    • length_scale — how quickly correlation decays with distance.
    • periodic_scale — how peaky the seasonal cycle is.
    • long_term_scale — how smooth the year-on-year trend is.

The covariance is separable: it factorises as a Kronecker product of a small spatial kernel and a small temporal kernel. This is the structural assumption that makes the maths tractable — we never need to handle a \((n \cdot n_t) \times (n \cdot n_t)\) matrix as a whole.

The parameters we want to learn from the data are \(f\), \(\mu_s\), \(\theta\), and \(r\). “Learn” here means: get a sample of values that is representative of the posterior distribution \(p(f, \mu, \theta, r \mid y)\). Once we have such a sample we can answer any question we want — the posterior mean for a missing week, a 95% credible interval, a forecast — by summarising the sample.

2. Why this is hard, and what we do instead

There are two reasons this is not a one-line maximum-likelihood problem.

Reason A: the latent field is enormous. For 1000 facilities and 150 weeks, \(f\) has 150,000 entries. Standard tools like Stan’s NUTS can sample over that many parameters in principle, but the geometry is awful (the latent field is strongly correlated by construction), and you spend most of your time tuning step sizes.

Reason B: the likelihood is not Gaussian. If \(y_{st}\) were Gaussian, we could write down the posterior of \(f\) in closed form and be done. The negative-binomial likelihood doesn’t admit that, so we’d usually fall back on a Laplace approximation. But Laplace can be badly biased when counts are small, which is exactly the regime where routine HF data lives.

The strategy weave uses sidesteps both problems:

  • Treat the model as a Gibbs sampler: cycle through the parameters, updating each block from its conditional posterior given everything else.
  • Make every block either exactly conjugate (closed-form Gaussian or conjugate update) or a simple one-dimensional sampler (univariate slice, random-walk Metropolis).
  • The hard block — the 150,000-dim latent field \(f\) — becomes conjugate-Gaussian after a clever data augmentation (next section), so it’s just one matrix-free linear solve per sweep.

The result is a sampler with no hand-tuning, exact (no Laplace anywhere), and laptop-feasible for problem sizes in the thousands of sites and hundreds of weeks.

3. The Pólya-Gamma identity

This is the only piece of maths you need to keep in mind. Everything else follows from it.

The negative-binomial density, written with the linear predictor \(\eta = \psi - \log r\), has an awkward denominator:

\[ P(y \mid r, \eta) \;\propto\; \frac{e^{y \eta}}{(1 + e^{\eta})^{\,y + r}}. \]

The denominator is what stops the conditional posterior of \(\eta\) from being Gaussian. The Pólya-Gamma identity (Polson, Scott, & Windle 2013) says: there is an auxiliary random variable \(\omega\) with the Pólya-Gamma distribution such that

\[ \frac{e^{y \eta}}{(1 + e^{\eta})^{\,y + r}} = \int_0^\infty \exp\!\left(\kappa \eta - \tfrac{\omega}{2}\eta^2\right)\, p_{\mathrm{PG}}(\omega \mid y + r, 0)\, d\omega, \qquad \kappa = \frac{y - r}{2}. \]

Read the integrand on the right as a function of \(\eta\). With \(\omega\) held fixed it is exactly a Gaussian in \(\eta\), with mean \(\kappa/\omega\) and precision \(\omega\). The negative-binomial likelihood has been re-expressed as a Gaussian observation of \(\eta\), conditional on a latent variable \(\omega\) we can sample efficiently.

Practical consequence: introducing one \(\omega\) per observed cell turns the latent-field block from “non-Gaussian likelihood, no closed form” into “Gaussian likelihood, closed-form conditional”. The whole rest of the sampler is built on this.

The BayesLogit::rpg function provides fast vectorised draws from the Pólya-Gamma distribution.

4. Inside one sweep

A single sweep of the sampler updates every parameter once, in the following order:

  1. \(\omega\) given \((f, \mu, \theta, r, y)\)
  2. \(f\) given \((\omega, \mu, \theta, r, y)\)
  3. \(\mu\) given \((\omega, f, r, y)\)
  4. \(\theta\) given \(f\)
  5. \(r\) given \((y, \psi)\)

We’ll go through each block, explaining both the what (the conditional distribution) and the how (the algorithm).

4.1 Block 1: \(\omega \mid \mathrm{rest}\) — the auxiliary refresh

For each observed cell \(i\) we compute \(\eta_i = \psi_i - \log r = f_i + \mu_{s(i)} - \log r\), and draw

\[ \omega_i \sim \mathrm{PG}(y_i + r,\; \eta_i). \]

This is one call to BayesLogit::rpg per sweep, vectorised over all observed cells. It is cheap (microseconds per draw) and trivially parallel.

Intuition: \(\omega_i\) is the “weight” the Gaussian-disguised likelihood will give to cell \(i\) in the next block. Cells where the likelihood is sharp get large \(\omega\); cells where the likelihood is flat get small \(\omega\).

4.2 Block 2: \(f \mid \mathrm{rest}\) — the heavy block

After conditioning on \(\omega\), the latent field has a Gaussian conditional posterior. Writing \(S\) for the matrix that selects the observed cells out of the full \(n \times n_t\) grid, and \(\Omega = \mathrm{diag}(\omega)\):

\[ \Sigma_{\text{post}}^{-1} = K^{-1} + S^{\!\top} \Omega S, \qquad \mu_{\text{post}} = \Sigma_{\text{post}}\, S^{\!\top} \Omega\, (y^* - \mu), \]

where \(y^*_i = \kappa_i / \omega_i + \log r\) is the pseudo-observation the PG identity gave us.

We never form \(\Sigma_{\text{post}}\) — it’s a 150,000-dimensional dense matrix at production scale. Instead we use the perturbation sampler of Papandreou & Yuille (2010) / Bhattacharya et al. (2016), which produces an exact draw from the conditional Gaussian with one matrix-free linear solve:

  1. Sample a prior draw \(u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, K)\). Because \(K\) is Kronecker-separable, this is cheap (the existing quick_mvnorm does it in two Cholesky factorisations of small \(n \times n\) and \(n_t \times n_t\) matrices).
  2. Sample an observation-noise draw \(e_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\omega_i)\) at each observed cell.
  3. Solve, by preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG), the small observation-space system \[(K + S^{\!\top}\Omega^{-1}S)\,\gamma = (y^* - \mu) - S u - e.\]
  4. Return \(f = u + K\, S^{\!\top}\gamma\).

The arithmetic to show this \(f\) has exactly the right Gaussian distribution is a few lines of algebra. The intuition: \(u\) is the right covariance but the wrong mean; the PCG solve is the precise correction that moves it to the posterior mean while preserving the covariance.

Why PCG is fast here. PCG only needs two things: a way to multiply by the system matrix, and a “preconditioner” — a fast approximation to its inverse. The system matrix never needs to exist as a matrix; the multiply is a sequence of small operations using the Kronecker structure. The preconditioner uses the eigendecomposition of the small \(K_{\text{space}}\) and \(K_{\text{time}}\) kernels — also cheap. Typical convergence is in 50–150 iterations even on the production-scale problem.

4.3 Block 3: \(\mu_s \mid \mathrm{rest}\) — per-site Gaussian

The pseudo-observation \(y^*_i\) is a Gaussian observation of \(\psi_i = f_i + \mu_{s(i)}\) with precision \(\omega_i\). Subtracting \(f_i\) gives a Gaussian observation of \(\mu_{s(i)}\) alone. Combined with the \(\mathcal{N}(m_0, v_0)\) prior on \(\mu_s\), the conditional posterior is closed-form Normal per site:

\[ \text{prec} = \frac{1}{v_0} + \sum_{i\, \in\, \text{site } s} \omega_i, \qquad \text{mean} = \frac{m_0/v_0 + \sum_{i\, \in\, \text{site } s} \omega_i (y^*_i - f_i)}{\text{prec}}. \]

Implemented as two tapply accumulations over sites and one vector of rnorm draws — fast and trivially correct.

4.4 Block 4: \(\theta \mid f\) — slice on the kernel hyperparameters

The three kernel hyperparameters \(\theta = (\ell_s, \ell_p, \ell_t)\) each get a univariate slice sampler on the log scale. The target is the GP prior log-density of \(f\) at the current value:

\[ \log p(\theta \mid f) = \log p(\theta) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log|K(\theta)| - \tfrac{1}{2}\, f^{\!\top} K(\theta)^{-1} f. \]

We can compute both \(\log|K|\) and \(f^{\!\top} K^{-1} f\) cheaply because of the Kronecker structure: eigendecompose the small \(K_{\text{space}}\) and \(K_{\text{time}}\) separately, and the log-determinant is a sum of the log eigenvalues, while the quadratic form is a sum of squared entries of the rotated \(f\) divided by the eigenvalues.

The slice sampler itself (Neal 2003) needs no tuning. It picks a horizontal “slice” at a uniform height under the log-target, finds an interval bracketing the current value where the target is above the slice, and then samples uniformly from that interval (shrinking on rejection). Working on \(\log \theta\) gives well-behaved stepping-out and keeps every value positive.

This is the slowest block. Each accepted slice move requires an \(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition of the spatial kernel, which is a few seconds at \(n = 1000\).

4.5 Block 5: \(r \mid \mathrm{rest}\) — random-walk Metropolis

The NB dispersion \(r\) has no conjugate update, but it’s a single positive scalar and the posterior is well-behaved. We use a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings update on \(\log r\):

  1. Propose \(\log r' = \log r + \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)\).
  2. Accept with probability \[\min\!\left\{1,\; \frac{p(y \mid \psi, r') p(r')}{p(y \mid \psi, r) p(r)} \cdot \frac{r'}{r}\right\}.\] (The trailing factor is the Jacobian for working on the log scale.)

The proposal scale \(s\) is tuned adaptively during burn-in using Robbins-Monro: nudge \(\log s\) in the direction that brings the acceptance rate toward the gold-standard 0.44 for 1-D random walks.

This is the only block with any “tuning”, and we hide it inside burn-in.

5. Multiple chains, diagnostics, and posterior output

A single chain is enough for point estimates but not enough to be confident. To detect non-convergence we run multiple chains in parallel with deliberately different starting points, and compare them.

5.1 Overdispersed initial values

With n_chains > 1, fit_bayes() starts each chain after the first at random draws from the priors of \(\theta\) and \(r\). The first chain still uses sensible deterministic starts so it converges quickly. The other chains are “overdispersed” — different enough that if they all collapse onto the same posterior region, we can trust the result.

5.2 Rhat (Gelman-Rubin diagnostic)

For each parameter we compute the potential scale reduction factor \(\hat R\). Informally:

\[ \hat R \approx \sqrt{\frac{\text{total variance across all chains}}{\text{average within-chain variance}}}. \]

If the chains have all found the same distribution, the numerator and denominator are equal and \(\hat R = 1\). Values bigger than \(\sim 1.05\) are a sign chains haven’t merged — either the sampler is broken, the posterior is multimodal, or the chains need more sweeps.

summary.weave_bayes() reports \(\hat R\) for length_scale, periodic_scale, long_term_scale, and r automatically when n_chains > 1.

5.3 Effective sample size (ESS)

Chains are autocorrelated, so 1000 sweeps don’t give 1000 independent posterior draws. The effective sample size is the number of equivalent independent draws. Aim for ESS \(\geq 400\) per parameter for anything you want to quote a credible interval on; \(\geq 100\) for exploration. coda::effectiveSize computes this from the trace.

5.4 Storage of the latent field

Storing \(f\) at every sweep is a memory bomb (150k × 5000 sweeps × 3 chains ≈ 18 GB at 8 bytes/double). By default fit_bayes() keeps only a running mean and variance of \(f\) (Welford’s algorithm; numerically stable and \(O(N)\) per update). For posterior-predictive draws you can opt in to keeping a thinned set of full \(f\) vectors — the default of 100 evenly-spaced samples per chain is plenty for downstream plotting.

6. Putting it together: a worked example

Below is the minimal call that exercises everything we just discussed. For more depth see the full walkthrough at implementation/test_walkthrough.R.

library(weave)
source(system.file("../implementation/simulation.R", package = "weave"))

# Simulate some HF count data.
set.seed(42)
n  <- 30; nt <- 104
coords <- data.frame(
  id  = factor(1:n),
  lat = runif(n, 0, 5),
  lon = runif(n, 0, 5),
  mu  = log(runif(n, 10, 80))
)
space_k <- space_kernel(coords, length_scale = 2)
time_k  <- time_kernel(1:nt, periodic_scale = 1, long_term_scale = 200)
true    <- simulate_data(n, nt, coords, space_k, time_k, r = 15)
obs     <- observed_data(true, p_one = 0.2, p_switch = 0.3)

# Fit.
fit <- fit_bayes(
  obs,
  n_sweeps = 1000, burnin = 400,
  n_chains = 3,
  store_f  = "thin"
)

# Diagnostics + posterior summary with Rhat.
summary(fit)

What to look at after the fit:

  1. Print the object: chain count, sweeps, observation count, median PCG iterations per sweep, MH acceptance for \(r\) per chain.
  2. Summary: posterior medians and 95% intervals for the kernel parameters and \(r\), plus \(\hat R\).
  3. Posterior predictive: posterior_predict(fit) gives one count-scale draw per stored MCMC sample at every cell. Coverage on held-out cells should be close to nominal.
  4. Run multiple chains: the only honest way to know the sampler has converged is to see chains overlap and \(\hat R\) near 1.

7. When the sampler is unhappy

A short field guide:

  • \(\hat R > 1.1\) on long_term_scale after a moderate run: usually just slow mixing on a weakly identified parameter. Add more sweeps before worrying about model misspecification.
  • MH acceptance for \(r\) outside \([0.15, 0.6]\) after burn-in: the adaptive scale didn’t converge. Re-run with a longer burn-in.
  • PCG iteration count climbs across sweeps: this can happen when \(\omega\) becomes very heterogeneous (e.g. small \(r\) + lots of zero cells). Increase pcg_maxit or relax pcg_tol.
  • Posterior predictive coverage well under 95%: the model is mis- specified. The two usual culprits are (a) site-level deviation from the GP smoothness assumption — some sites really are different — and (b) the wrong period (e.g. you used period = 52 for monthly data).
